As the West Turns Increasingly "Right," Where Are China's Opportunities?

Published: 2026-10-10 | Words: 2.6k | Reading time ≈ 13 min | Views:  | Translations:

  • Es

When watching Western news, we often hear terms like “left-wing government” and “right-wing party.”

Diversity, equality, and social welfare are often associated with the left; while tradition, religion, tax cuts, and stricter immigration are frequently linked to the right.

What do these labels actually mean? Why do seemingly disparate issues like taxes, immigration, abortion, and religion always appear in the same political debates?

As the right wing expands its influence in multiple countries, how should China respond?

Let me clarify one point first: this article discusses the general tendencies of the contemporary Western left and right, not strict definitions of every political party. Conditions vary by country, and the propositions of moderates and radicals differ in degree and implementation.

1. Left and Right: Originally Just a Difference in Seating#

The terms “left-wing” and “right-wing” originated during the French Revolution.

In 1789, representatives advocating for the expansion of parliamentary power sat on the left, while those supporting the maintenance of the king’s power sat on the right. Over time, these seating positions gradually became the names of political stances.

Over two hundred years later, the debate has expanded from royal power versus parliament to wealth distribution, social security, religious traditions, and individual rights.

Today, the left generally emphasizes equality, reform, and inclusivity; the right generally emphasizes tradition, order, market competition, and personal responsibility.

To understand them, we can start with a few things directly related to the lives of ordinary people.

2. How to Distribute Money: Fair Distribution vs. Market Incentives#

In the face of the wealth gap, the left places more emphasis on the regulatory role of the government.

In the view of the left, a person’s situation depends not only on their efforts but is also influenced by family, education, and social resources. Therefore, high-income earners should bear a greater tax burden, and the government should strengthen public services and labor protection so that the fruits of economic growth can benefit more people.

The right emphasizes market competition, private property, and personal responsibility, advocating for tax cuts and deregulation to encourage investment and entrepreneurship.

The logic is: if it is easier to run a business and capital is more willing to invest, more jobs and income will be created. Lowering the tax burden on businesses and investors is intended to enhance the driving force for wealth creation.

The left worries more that wealth will grow but become concentrated in the hands of a few; the right worries more that excessive taxation and regulation will cause investment and business operations to lose momentum.

What the two sides are debating is how much the government should intervene and how to balance fairness and efficiency.

3. What to Do When Facing Difficulties: Social Safety Nets vs. Personal Self-Reliance#

When a person is unemployed, sick, elderly, or falls into poverty, how much responsibility should society bear?

The left pays more attention to the basic security of low-income earners and vulnerable groups, believing that illness, unemployment, and poverty cannot be entirely attributed to a lack of personal effort. Public healthcare, unemployment benefits, housing assistance, and pension security should form society’s basic safety net.

The right emphasizes self-reliance, believing that individuals and families should first be responsible for their own lives. Government assistance should be subject to conditions such as eligibility checks and work requirements to control public spending and reduce long-term welfare dependency.

The left is more concerned about whether security is sufficient and whether anyone is left behind; the right is more concerned about whether the budget can afford it and whether welfare undermines the motivation to work.

The healthcare debate in the US is a case in point. Obamacare (the Affordable Care Act) allowed more people to obtain health insurance by expanding Medicaid and providing premium subsidies; Trump and the Republican Party pushed to cut some healthcare spending and tighten eligibility conditions.

For ordinary people, what these debates ultimately affect is: whether there is income during unemployment, whether one can afford to see a doctor when sick, and whether there is security in old age.

4. Who Decides How to Live: Personal Choice vs. Traditional Norms#

In terms of social culture, the left emphasizes diversity, inclusivity, and equal rights, focusing on the rights of groups such as women, ethnic minorities, homosexuals, and transgender people. They advocate for reducing discrimination and expanding the space for personal choice.

The right emphasizes traditional families, existing ethics, and social order, believing that common cultural and behavioral norms are important foundations for maintaining social stability. Regarding changes to marriage, gender, and family systems, the right tends to uphold traditional arrangements.

The left emphasizes that as long as a person does not harm others, they should have the right to live according to their own choices.

The right emphasizes that personal choices also need to consider traditional norms, as well as the stability of the family and society.

Both sides talk about “freedom,” but the focus is different. On social issues, the left emphasizes the freedom of lifestyle and identity choices; on economic and religious issues, the right emphasizes operational autonomy, property rights, and freedom of belief.

Religion is an important clue to understanding this divide.

The left generally emphasizes the separation of church and state, believing that public policy should not be dominated by any single religious doctrine. The right is more closely connected to conservative religious forces, tending to bring traditional religious ethics into family, education, and reproductive policies.

Women’s abortion rights are a typical example.

The left emphasizes women’s bodily autonomy, health, and practical circumstances, supporting the guarantee of legal and safe abortion services.

The right emphasizes the protection of fetal life and tends to restrict abortion. This is closely related to the influence of religious conservatism: conservative Christian ethics emphasize the sanctity of life, which in turn influences legal propositions.

Therefore, the abortion debate involves both how rights are weighed and the extent to which religious ethics should constrain everyone’s lives.

5. Who Does the Country Accept: Immigration vs. Common Identity#

On the issue of immigration, the left emphasizes humanitarian protection, equal treatment, and cultural diversity, and takes a more positive view of immigrants’ contributions to the labor force, the economy, and social vitality.

The right emphasizes border control, entry qualifications, social carrying capacity, and national cultural identity, tending to tighten immigration policies and set higher requirements for language, employment, and social integration.

The left focuses more on whether immigrants are treated fairly and whether society can accept different cultures; the right focuses more on whether the scale of immigration is controllable and whether newcomers can adapt to local rules and lifestyles.

The common identity emphasized by the right often includes the native language, historical traditions, and Christian culture. When this identity turns exclusionary, it may turn skin color, ethnicity, and religion into criteria for judging whether a person “truly belongs to this country.”

Therefore, the immigration debate involves not only employment and population but also how a country defines its members.

6. How to Face the World: Global Cooperation vs. Nation First#

In international relations, the left generally emphasizes multilateral cooperation, tending to address issues like climate change, refugees, and public health through international organizations, common rules, and transnational coordination.

The nationalist right emphasizes national sovereignty and prioritizing the nation, focusing on whether international arrangements directly benefit their own country, and opposing taking on excessive costs or accepting too many constraints for the sake of cooperation.

Trump is a clear representative of this path. He emphasizes “America First,” striving for national interests through tariffs, manufacturing reshoring, and renegotiating trade arrangements.

This reflects trade protectionism and a unilateralism that prefers to rely on national strength to take action.

The divergence between the two sides lies in how they understand national interests: whether to place greater value on common rules and long-term cooperation, or on national autonomy and direct benefits.

7. As Right-Wing Influence Rises, How Should China Respond?#

In recent years, nationalism and right-wing populist forces have expanded their influence in several countries. The Trump path in the US and the growth of right-wing parties in Europe are both manifestations of this change.

The cost of living, immigration, public safety, and dissatisfaction with globalization have become important issues for the right wing to mobilize voters, putting the left and traditional centrists in some countries under pressure.

China needs to prepare for an international environment where “nation first,” border control, and trade protection are more highly valued.

“Nation first” governments may raise tariffs, restrict investment, and push for supply chain reshoring. Chinese enterprises need to diversify their market distribution, reduce reliance on a single market, and improve their ability to cope with policy changes.

When dealing with governments that prioritize employment and industry, China can propose actionable cooperation plans with measurable returns centered around manufacturing, infrastructure, energy, and technology application, making the local benefits brought by such cooperation more tangible.

At the same time, stable international rules must be maintained. Corporate investment and long-term trade require a predictable environment and cannot rely solely on a single deal that could change at any moment.

China’s response needs to combine pragmatic cooperation, risk diversification, and the strengthening of its own capabilities.

8. Tightened Immigration May Bring Talent Opportunities for China#

In my view, an important opportunity that the rightward shift in Western politics might bring is a change in the pattern of talent mobility.

A major factor in why the United States is so powerful is its long-term attraction of talent from all over the world, providing them with opportunities for research, entrepreneurship, and development. Meanwhile, China’s rapid development over the past few decades has largely benefited from the massive number of engineering and technical personnel trained during this period.

According to statistics from the National Foundation for American Policy, between 2000 and 2025, 40% of US Nobel Prize winners in the three categories of Physics, Chemistry, and Physiology or Medicine were immigrants.

The same is true in the business world. Google co-founder Sergey Brin is from the former Soviet Union, Elon Musk was born in South Africa, and Jensen Huang immigrated to the US from Taiwan. Steve Jobs was born in the US, but his biological father was a Syrian immigrant.

What immigrants bring is not just population, but also knowledge, technology, entrepreneurial ability, and the developmental potential of the next generation.

If tightening immigration further affects studying abroad, scientific research, employment, and long-term residency, leaving talent with a lack of stable expectations for the future, the appeal of the US to international talent could decline.

The losses may not be immediately apparent, but they could manifest years later as a reduction in research outcomes, startup enterprises, and industrial opportunities.

For China, this is a window of opportunity to compete for talent.

However, talent will not automatically walk into China just because the US closes a door. China needs to offer attractive conditions: stable and transparent rules, convenient residency arrangements, a good environment for research and entrepreneurship, and public services that allow talent and their families to live with peace of mind.

This includes both attracting overseas Chinese talent back home and attracting talent from other countries to China. The key is making talent willing to come, stay, and fully utilize their strengths.

9. How Might Religious Conservatism Affect Long-Term Development?#

Beyond talent, there is another issue worth paying attention to: will conservative religious forces use political power to compress the space for rational exploration?

Europe’s own history offers a cautionary tale.

The ancient Greeks—Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle—left behind a tradition of understanding the world through questioning, reasoning, and observation. The natural philosophy among these was an important source for the later development of science. The Hellenistic world and Roman civilization also once boasted highly developed cities, trade, engineering, and knowledge systems.

But in the late 4th century AD, after the Roman Empire established Christianity as the state religion, Europe experienced a long period of urban decline, trade contraction, and political fragmentation. During the long Middle Ages that followed, the Church profoundly influenced education, culture, and social life, and religious authority became a constraint that ideological exploration had to face.

When doctrine holds the power to adjudicate knowledge, researchers must not only prove their viewpoints but also prove that they do not violate the faith.

This constraint can sometimes be enforced in extremely severe ways.

In 1600, Giordano Bruno was burned at the stake in Rome after being deemed a heretic. His ideas involved cosmology, philosophy, and theology; his case reflects how religious authority used criminal punishment to penalize those who challenged dogma.

In 1633, Galileo faced the Inquisition for supporting the view that the Earth moves, and was subsequently placed under long-term house arrest. This demonstrates even more directly the interference of religious authority in scientific research: judgments about the natural world were ultimately handed over to religious tribunals to decide.

Europe’s path to modernity went through multiple transformations, including the Renaissance, the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution, and the Enlightenment. A key shift was that observation, experimentation, and evidence gradually gained independent status, and scientific questions were no longer solely subject to the adjudication of religious authority.

This history reveals a fundamental conflict: science allows for questioning, testing, and revision, while once religious dogma possesses coercive adjudicative power, it may view questioning as an offense and new insights as heresy.

This is also related to the left-right debate of today.

The right has close ties with conservative religious forces. When the right is in power, these forces may gain greater policy influence. If doctrine further becomes the supreme basis for legislation, education, and scientific research, state governance may tilt toward religiousization.

In my opinion, if a country tightens its entry points for talent on the one hand, and allows extreme religious conservative forces to restrict intellectual exploration on the other, it could erode its own innovation advantages from both directions.

10. How Can China Turn Opportunities into Advantages?#

China needs to combine attracting talent with respecting science.

Attracting talent requires stable systems, ample development opportunities, and good living conditions; utilizing the role of talent requires safeguarding the space for exploration, ensuring that research is based on evidence, and ensuring that abilities are recognized.

China should also strengthen secular governance and restrict the interference of religious dogma in public power, school education, and scientific research.

At the same time, scientific education should be strengthened so that the public understands how evidence is generated, how conclusions are tested, and why understandings need to be revised.

State governance should also involve more scientists and engineers with scientific literacy and practical experience in decision-making, emphasizing data, professional assessment, and actual outcomes. This should be combined with legal, economic, and social knowledge to establish mechanisms capable of discovering problems and correcting mistakes.

The left-right debate ultimately boils down to how a society distributes wealth, treats different people, determines the boundaries of knowledge, and interacts with the world.

Faced with the rising influence of the right wing, China not only needs to cope with trade protection and policy changes but can also proactively vie for talent and cooperation opportunities.

China cannot determine the political direction of other countries. But China can decide whether it is open to talent itself, whether it supports free exploration, and whether its public decision-making is more heavily based on evidence.

The true opportunity lies not just in what others have lost, but even more in what China can provide for talent, knowledge, and creation.
